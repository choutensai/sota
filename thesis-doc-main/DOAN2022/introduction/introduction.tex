\section{Introduction}
Robotic manipulation \cite{mason2018toward} is a fundamental task in robotics, enabling robots to physically interact with their environment across a wide range of applications, including manufacturing, logistics, and autonomous systems. Manipulation strategies can be broadly classified into two categories: prehensile and non-prehensile manipulation, depending on the nature of the contact interactions between the robot and the manipulated object. While prehensile manipulation \cite{bicchi2000robotic} involves establishing and maintaining a stable grasp on an object, typically through multiple contact points, non-prehensile manipulation \cite{lynch1996nonprehensile,lynch1999dynamic} achieves object motion only through contact interactions such as pushing, sliding, and rolling. Among these techniques, pushing \cite{mason1986mechanics} is widely employed as a simple yet effective manipulation primitive for achieving desired object poses in planar environments. Unlike grasp-based approaches, pushing does not require form or force closure, making it particularly suitable manipulating objects that are challenging to grasp. 

While single-robot pushing provides a simple and effective means of object manipulation, its capabilities may be constrained by the limited force capacity and available contact locations of an individual robot. To address these limitations, multi-robot pushing \cite{ozaki1997distributed,tuci2018cooperative} employs a team of robots to cooperatively manipulate an object by applying forces at different contact locations, overcoming overcoming the limitations of individual robots in terms of force capacity and manipulation flexibility. Rosenfelder et al. \cite{rosenfelder2024force} developed a unified framework for cooperative pushing, integrating formation optimization, force allocation, and distributed control. This comprehensive approach provides a systematic solution for coordinating multiple robots to achieve reliable and efficient object transportation.

The inertial parameters of an object, particularly its CoM, play a crucial role in determining its dynamic response to applied pushing forces. While some adaptive pushing control methods do not require prior knowledge of the object's CoM \cite{wang2016kinematic,nguyen2026dnpush}, accurate estimation of its location can provides valuable insight into the object's physical characteristics, facilitating more informed and reliable manipulation planning. In particular, CoM information can guide the determination of appropriate contact configurations and pushing strategies to produce the desired object motion. For instance, Cosgun et al. \cite{cosgun2011push} employed the object's CoM as a reference for initializing gripper placement when planning linear pushing actions. Similarly, Nguyen et al. \cite{nguyen2024distributed} introduced a distributed cascade force control strategy for cooperative object transportation, assuming that the robots are positioned on opposite sides of the projection of the object's CoM onto the contact edge. Despite its importance in manipulation planning and control, the object's CoM is often assumed to be known or approximated from geometric information under a uniform mass distribution. However, these simplifications may not adequately capture he object's true characteristics, potentially compromising the accuracy of CoM estimates.
